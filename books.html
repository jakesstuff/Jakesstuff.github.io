Star Trek: Faces of Fire by Michael Jan Friedman (1992)

    Star Trek: Faces of Fire is a sort of prequel to the second and third Star Trek movies. We meet a young David Marcus, son of Kirk, 10 years before the events of the second film. We also get to see the ascension of Kruge, the Klingon captain of the third. The book has two plots. While Spock remains on the research base for the Genesis device (only called the G-7 here), Kirk and crew go to a planet to thwart an emerging civil war between two religious groups.

    Despite the opportunity, the book fails to give much characterization or personality to Carol, the mother of Kirk’s son David. She’s a scientist who takes pride in her job, that’s about it. The Klingon part of the story (a ship occupies the base to acquire the G-7) is interesting, but it was frustrating to see how bumbling and ineffective they were.

A    As I’m discovering is typical with these early-90s Star Trek books, there are a couple times when characters are described as being black to differentiate them from the otherwise default, and never described as such, white characters. Also typically, Kirk’s crew are sycophantic and incapable of handling things of their own accord. At a point, Kirk and Scotty go missing, Spock is on the research planet, so who’s in charge of the ship? Seemingly nobody. The most Doctor McCoy does is muse to an ambassador his concern about the situation. It is fortunate things work out because no one seems to have much initiative to try and rescue their missing captain on the planet below.

    All in all, another disappointing story from Friedman and I think the last I read of his. I think this story would have been so much better if the entire religious civil war side plot was cut out, and we just got to see some Starfleet-on-Klingon action.

Review written 3/13/2026

Rating: 0/10

    Star Trek: Rules of Engagement is soooo boring. The plot follows the Enterprise being dispatched to evacuate Starfleet personnel from a planet that has recently been couped by generic “terrorists”. Flowers For Algernon by Daniel Keyes (1966)

Review written 2/15/2026

Rating: 10/10

    Flowers For Algernon is nothing short of the best book I have read at this point in my life. It was recommended to me by my friend Dominic. It is about a man, Charlie Gordon, who has an intellectual disability. Through a revolutionary treatment, he his gifted with an IQ that soon surpasses that of everyone around him, for a short time.

    The characters are real. They’re well rounded. They make mistakes, and they make amends. Every character is a human being with flaws and successes and dreams and limitations. This story explores these themes, of what it means to have a good life, and what it means to live.

    This story is so excruciatingly, satisfyingly, tragic. Be prepared for this when you read it, which, you absolutely must. About 30 pages from the end of the book, Charlie Gordon writes a reportive analysis of the medical procedure he underwent. I implore you to pause here, and when you have time, read every page after in succession without pausing.


Star Trek TNG: Reunion by Michael Jan Friedman (1991)

Review written 2/08/2026

Rating: 4/10

    Star Trek TNG: Reunion was, disappointing. It introduces the members of Picard’s old crew when he was the captain of the Stargazer. One of them is set to be anointed as the new ruler of their world, and the Enterprise is tasked with transporting him and his chosen entourage (Picard and the alien’s best friends from the Stargazer). A mystery strikes, as someone begins sabotaging the ship and attempting to assassinate the old Stargazer crew members!

    This really got me hooked, and for the first half of the 300+ page book, I was really into it. But new plot points get added that, really do not need to be there, at all. We get to see a little backstory from Jack Crusher as Beverly listens to some old audio recordings (which conflicts with Wesley’s comment in the series about how sometimes he forgets his father’s voice). Again, another story where the good doctor gets as much character development as she did in the series: next to none. The mystery assassin plot turned out a little disappointing as well.

    The book was, okay. I was so excited and I was really let down. It says it was a bestseller on the cover, and I think a lot of people were interested in it because of the backstory of the Stargazer’s final moments. Even if you’re going to pick it up for that purpose, I don’t think you’ll be satisfied with what you get out of it. I don’t recommend this book.


Landscape With Invisible Hand by M.T. Anderson (2017)

Review written 1/31/2026

Rating: 7/10

    Landscape With Invisible Hand is a science fiction story about an alien race that has, essentially, beat humanity in the game of capitalism. We follow a teen artist as he experiences this new modern era.

    I watched the movie that was based on this book first, and if you have done the same: they are completely different stories. The movie enraptured me with it’s shock moments and spectacle. The book is more real. It's darker, and sadder, but ends on a high(er) note.

    I enjoyed the bite size chapters, and I read the book in a single evening. As the author intended, it really felt like each chapter was painting a scene in this world. The sentences read like brushstrokes.

    The book was full of themes, some laid out more cleanly than others. As with the best science fiction, the story is about humanity, not the aliens. Commentary on capitalism, post-war economics, power, the surrendering of cultural pride in the face of colonialism, poverty and the callousness it induces, and by-your-bootstraps Americanism. It is a young adult novel, so I don’t blame its lack of subtlety in regards to many of these issues, but it makes the story no less entertaining.

    I recommend this book. I think it's a great and entertaining read for anyone.


Star Trek TNG: Here There Be Dragons by John Peel (1993)

Review written 1/31/2026

Rating: 6/10

    Star Trek TNG: Here There Be Dragons is a really fun book. It made me laugh a lot at it’s cheesiness, and really felt like a screenplay right out of Season 1. Picard and crew must go down to thwart the plot of an international trophy hunting gang, corrupting the village on a planet of transplanted medieval citizenry by “The Preservers” first introduced in The Original Series.

    We got to see some minor characters have a time to shine, including Reg Barclay and Ro Laren (including an unusual and unnecessary moment of her in the nude). However, typical to most of TNG, Doctor Crusher and Troi each get very minor roles (Crusher is a higher rank than LaForge, why is she not acting captain?). Although LIEUTENANT Miles O’Brien is part of the away team, it did turn into a typical Picard-Data lead plot on the surface of the planet. Riker and Troi doing their own thing getting into trouble (the latter mostly just attending to the plans of the former), and Ro Laren existing mostly to help out in the final moments.

    The characters felt well written as themselves (LaForge felt a little generic to me), but I think the author introduced too many to manage at once and had to give most of them rather dull storylines. A fun and silly book if you find yourself enamored by Star Trek and pop-medieval history, but as said above, it reads like an early season episode with a fair share of minor disappointments in what could have been.

I didn't go out of my way to get this book, I got it in a $10-fill-a-box event from the Cheyenne library.

    Rating: 1/10 All Done Without Deductive Reasoning is about three car jackers in Texas who are finally caught by our protagonist, who is referred to multiple times as, "Woman Deputy Alice Fayde", and "formerly blonde red-head" (after she took her disguise off). Repeating himself is something Edson does frequently describing almost everything, and I think the story could be a third shorter if he cut out such. Alice is only present in the story because the criminals target women, so that's her explicit purpose for being in this sting operation, and fortunately, well trained in Karate to take them down. Alice is blessed by being one of only two characters (the other being a criminal begging for his life) who get's the chance to speak, and it is entirely telling the same criminal to not try anything, multiple times, while she threatens to shoot him in the groin (as women have trained to do after the court system refused to prosecute male assaulters, the author states). The criminals get gobs more backstory than our protagonist does, and she gets about the same as some random male deputy who shows up at the end with his 100 year old shotgun to stop the final escapee. When referring to the carjackers as hispanic, Edson insists on describing the third as "...looked like the anglo ancestor..." and similarly more than once. Also, the only officer who's race is described is also hispanic, because he has to tell the criminals their Miranda Rights in Spanish because, of course, despite speaking English, the three would get away with it in court by lying that they didn't. In the final scene as the final criminal is stopped in his getaway, a bystanders car is damaged. But no fret, because, as written in one of the many footnotes (mostly referencing Edson's other works), "Being what 'liberals' term a 'redneck' and, therefore, a stout believer in the enforcement of law and order, the owner raised no objections...". I laughed out loud at that part. Overall, it was quick paced and fun, yet ultimately a very basic fight sequence that shouldn't have taken 40 pages to tell.

Star Trek: Crossroad by Barbara Hambly (1994)

Review written 1/12/2026

Rating: 3/10

    Star Trek: Crossroad is a Star Trek The Original Series novel that takes place three months before the end of Kirk’s first five-year mission. This is the first Star Trek book I’ve ever read and it was pretty alright.

    Typical to a lot of Star Trek, there were a lot of characters with not a lot to do. Sulu gets a productive role in the story. Scotty gets really pushed to the side (I think as a way to explain why the characters can’t do certain tech stuff). Though Christine Chapel gets a good chance to shine, I don’t think she’s given as much of an opportunity as she could have been given. The book tries, but it doesn't resonate strongly.

    I was surprised by the supplication of lore concerning the neurodivergent in the Trek universe, and I was disappointed in how it was presented. I would expect that a book, written in the 90s, about an idyllic humanity hundreds of years in the future, would have a better vision for the neurodivergent than being tasked to menial labor. I found it highly unimaginative and rather offensive.

    I felt that the plot wasn’t very continuous. Yes, I could understand what was going on, but it felt like I was jumping from one segment of an outline to another, over and over. It didn’t bother me too much, but there were times that it didn’t feel very natural the way that events were progressing. The Enterprise also manages to get hijacked, not once, but twice! When it came to the ending: I was shocked. It was not a very good ending if you ask me. I don’t think a good story has to have a happy ending, but the book was full of tragedy, and I kept expecting something that would make it all worth it in the end, and that never came.

    It wasn’t a bad book, but it was a bit of a slog to me. Part of it was likely because I never found the Original Series one of my preferred crews, so I didn’t find the characters very compelling. Combined with the ending, and the unexpected actions of several of the characters, I was not satisfied at the end with the time I put into it.


Frankenstein by Mary Shelley (1818)

Review written 1/5/2026

Rating: 9/10

    Frankenstein is a classic, and for good reason. I’m thankful to have finally read it. A tragedy in the most classic way, where the creator of the famous monster has his entire life destroyed by his obsession.

    It’s dramatic, and I can really feel for the tragedy of the characters. A comparison to Moby Dick comes to mind, but unlike the monologuing of the former, Mary Shelley really keeps me enthralled and I never felt my mind wandering from the story. It’s progression felt natural. I loved the letters as part of the story. I loved the starting fake-out.

    When I think of drawbacks, it is vastly difficult to find one. It felt like a Greek epic. The characters were unique and compelling, and all very human. I found Victor’s self isolation very human and relatable, how easy it is to become lost in our own affairs.

    I can almost say, Shelley becomes lost in her own poetry. But that isn’t true. It’s not verbose, it adds such richness to the world and heightens the drama so much. It does sometimes become difficult to get through, however.

    It’s not a very long book, and I think would be best read over a single weekend. The story flows together so smoothly you won’t want to put it down. Do not let it’s age deter you. I highly recommend this book.


Monday The Rabbi Took Off by Harry Kemelman (1972)

Review written 12/22/2025

Rating: 3/10

    Monday The Rabbi Takes Off is the third in a series of mystery books starring the Rabbi David Small. It is very understandable as a stand-alone story and often I found myself forgetting that there was a backstory to it. Small takes a sabbatical to visit Israel, a place he has never been, as he contemplates his future: does he want to remain a rabbi to his loyal congregation, or retire to devote himself to personal research and relaxation. The story takes place in the present time, which in 1972 was only 5 years after the Six-Day War, and just 24 years after the founding of Israel. Rather late, about halfway through the book, there is an attack, and that is what the “mystery” revolves around.

    Although this is intended to be a mystery novel, the primary conflict is within David Small himself and his self-discovery. Most of the story revolves around him moving around parts of Israel, and that is what makes the book intriguing. The reader gets a glimpse of what it was like living in Israel at the time, primarily in Jerusalem, from a Jewish Israeli point of view.

    Alternating with Rabbi Small’s adventure, is the office politics of his congregation back home as they debate replacing him with a more “hip” and experienced Rabbi that the group has quickly grown fond of.

    The “C-Plot” of the book, is the mystery itself. The elements of anti-Arab racism are prevalent in this plot line. This is in particular demonstrated by a minor character who is easily tricked by the antagonists because of his liberal Americanized idealism. Kemelman sanitizes the conflict of the time, and intentionally flattens the characters and motivations involved in the attack. The characters refer to all non-Israeli citizens only as Arabs, relying on stereotypes and generalizations to make a clear good-versus-bad dynamic, despite the occasional attempts at depth.

    Ultimately, I did enjoy the book, but I don’t think most would. It’s a very slow burn, and the mystery portion of it is very dull. If you’re interested in seeing a romanticized “real life” of being Jewish in Israel in the early 70s, that would be it’s only redeeming aspect.
