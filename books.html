Star Trek: Crossroad by Barbara Hambly (1994)

Review written 1/12/2026

Rating: 5/10

    Star Trek: Crossroad is a Star Trek The Original Series novel that takes place three months before the end of Kirk’s first five-year mission. This is the first Star Trek book I’ve ever read and it was pretty alright.

    Typical to a lot of Star Trek, there were a lot of characters with not a lot to do. Sulu gets a productive role in the story. Scotty gets really pushed to the side (I think as a way to explain why the characters can’t do certain tech stuff). But nurse Christine Chapel gets a good chance to shine, but I don’t think she’s given as much as an opportunity as she could have been given.

    I was surprised by the supplication of lore concerning the neurodivergent in the Trek universe, and I was disappointed in how it was presented. I would expect that a book, written in the 90s, about an idyllic humanity hundreds of years in the future, would have a better vision for the neurodivergent than being tasked to menial labor. I found it highly unimaginative and, very likely, insulting to many people.

    I felt that the plot wasn’t very continuous. Yes, I could understand what was going on. But it felt like I was jumping from one segment of an outline to another, over and over. It didn’t both me too much, but there were times that it didn’t feel very natural the way that events were progressing. The Enterprise also manages to get hijacked, not once, but twice! When it came to the ending: I was shocked. It was not a very good ending if you ask me. I don’t think a good story has to have a happy ending, but the book was full of tragedy, and I kept expecting something that would make it all worth it in the end, and that never came.

    It wasn’t a bad book, but it was a bit of a slog to me. Part of it was likely because I never found the Original Series one of my preferred crews, so I didn’t find the characters very compelling. Combined with the ending, and the unexpected actions of several of the characters, I was not satisfied at the end with the time I put into it.


Frankenstein by Mary Shelley (1818)

Review written 1/5/2026

Rating: 9/10

    Frankenstein is a classic, and for good reason. I’m thankful to have finally read it. It’s dramatic, and I can really feel for the tragedy of the characters. A comparison to Moby Dick comes to mind, but unlike the monologuing of the former, Mary Shelley really keeps me enthralled and I never felt my mind wandering from the story. It’s progression felt natural. I loved the letters as part of the story. I loved the starting fake-out./p>

    When I think of drawbacks, it is vastly difficult to find one. It felt like a Greek epic. The characters were unique and compelling, and all very human. I found Victor’s self isolation very human and relatable, how easy it is to become lost in our own affairs.

     Why do I not grant this title a 10/10? Because I think very few stories can deserve such a title. I can almost say, Shelley becomes lost in her own poetry. But that isn’t true. It’s not verbose, it adds such richness to the world and heightens the drama so much. I force myself to find a critique and it comes as such: the movements of the monster often seemed deus-ex-machina-esque. However, I can easily suspend disbelief in the naturally inhuman survivability and tracking skills of the monster, and that serves as my only critique.

    Do not let it’s age deter you. I highly recommend this book.


Monday The Rabbi Took Off by Harry Kemelman (1972)

Review written 12/22/2025

Rating: 3/10

    Monday The Rabbi Takes Off is the third in a series of mystery books starring the Rabbi David Small. It is very understandable as a stand-alone story and often I found myself forgetting that there was a backstory to it. Small takes a sabbatical to visit Israel, a place he has never been, as he contemplates his future: does he want to remain a rabbi to his loyal congregation, or retire to devote himself to personal research and relaxation. The story takes place in the present time, which in 1972 was only 5 years after the Six-Day War, and just 24 years after the founding of Israel. Rather late, about halfway through the book, there is an attack, and that is what the “mystery” revolves around.

    Although this is intended to be a mystery novel, the primary conflict is within David Small himself and his self-discovery. Most of the story revolves around him moving around parts of Israel, and that is what makes the book intriguing. The reader gets a glimpse of what it was like living in Israel at the time, primarily in Jerusalem, from a Jewish Israeli point of view.

    Alternating with Rabbi Small’s adventure, is the office politics of his congregation back home as they debate replacing him with a more “hip” and experienced Rabbi that the group has quickly grown fond of.

    The “C-Plot” of the book, is the mystery itself. The elements of anti-Arab racism are prevalent in this plot line. This is in particular demonstrated by a minor character who is easily tricked by the antagonists because of his liberal Americanized idealism. Kemelman sanitizes the conflict of the time, and intentionally flattens the characters and motivations involved in the attack. The characters refer to all non-Israeli citizens only as Arabs, relying on stereotypes and generalizations to make a clear good-versus-bad dynamic, despite the occasional attempts at depth.

    Ultimately, I did enjoy the book, but I don’t think most would. It’s a very slow burn, and the mystery portion of it is very dull. If you’re interested in seeing a romanticized “real life” of being Jewish in Israel in the early 70s, that would be it’s only redeeming aspect.
